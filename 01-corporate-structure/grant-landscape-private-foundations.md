# Private Foundation & Corporate Grant Landscape — Deep Research

*Research date: 2026-04-06*

This is the detailed companion to `grant-landscape.md`, covering private foundations and corporate giving programs relevant to a new Georgia-based 501(c)(3) with two program areas: (1) EMS education access for high school programs and underserved communities, and (2) hospice/end-of-life care education and workforce development.

Each entry includes a realistic assessment of whether the funder is accessible to a brand-new organization or requires years of track record.

---

## TIER 1: REALISTIC FOR A NEW 501(c)(3) (Year 1-2)

These funders either explicitly welcome new organizations, have low minimums, or fund the specific niche this nonprofit occupies.

---

### Firehouse Subs Public Safety Foundation

**What they fund:** Life-saving equipment (AEDs, extrication tools, thermal cameras, chest compression devices, body armor, ATVs) and prevention education tools (fire safety, CPR training, DUI education, CO poisoning awareness). Also funds scholarships and continuing education for public safety careers.

**Why this matters for you:** The "prevention education" and "continuing education" categories are directly relevant. A grant for CPR/first aid training kits, AED trainers for high school EMS programs, or EMS education materials could fit.

**Typical award sizes:** Maximum $40,000 per request. Foundation suggests requests fall between $15,000-$25,000.

**Eligibility:** Fire departments, law enforcement, EMS organizations, municipal/state organizations, public safety nonprofits, schools. A new 501(c)(3) focused on EMS education is eligible. Applicants should ideally be within 60 miles of a Firehouse Subs restaurant, but the Board considers rural/volunteer departments regardless of location. Georgia has many Firehouse Subs locations, so proximity is not an issue.

**Application process:** Entirely digital through grants.firehousesubs.com. Maximum of 600 applications accepted per quarter, and they fill fast. Applications open at 10 AM ET on a quarterly schedule:
- Q2 2026: January 8, 2026
- Q3 2026: April 2, 2026
- Q4 2026: July 9, 2026

**Reapplication:** Must wait 3 years after a grant approval before reapplying.

**Realistic assessment:** HIGH PROBABILITY. This is one of the most accessible grants for a new EMS education nonprofit. The application is straightforward, the amounts are meaningful for a startup, and EMS education falls squarely in scope. Apply in the first quarter you can. Focus the request on tangible education tools (training manikins, AED trainers, curriculum materials) for high school EMS programs.

**Sources:** [Firehouse Subs Foundation](https://firehousesubsfoundation.org/) | [Grant Portal](https://grants.firehousesubs.com/)

### FEMA — Assistance to Firefighters Grant (AFG)

**What they fund:** Equipment, training, health/wellness programs for fire departments and non-affiliated EMS organizations. Covers training to recognized standards, personal protective equipment, facility improvements, emergency vehicles, and health/wellness programs.

**Why this matters for you:** A 501(c)(3) EMS education nonprofit qualifies as a "non-affiliated EMS organization" if it provides EMS-related services and is not hospital-affiliated. Training and education components are explicitly eligible activities.

**Typical award sizes:** Varies widely, from $10,000 to over $1 million depending on project scope. Smaller training-focused grants are common.

**Eligibility:** Fire departments and non-affiliated EMS organizations (public or private nonprofit providing medical transport/training, not hospital-affiliated). The nonprofit would need to demonstrate it serves a community EMS education function.

**Application process:** Annual application through FEMA's grant portal. FY24 application period: May 23 - July 3, 2025. Check grants.gov for FY25/26 NOFO.

**Realistic assessment:** MODERATE. Worth pursuing but competitive. The nonprofit would need a clear narrative about how its education programs serve underserved communities' EMS capabilities. Stronger if partnered with actual fire/EMS departments that can co-sign the need.

**Sources:** [FEMA AFG Program](https://www.fema.gov/grants/preparedness/firefighters/assistance-grants) | Help Desk: 866-274-0960

### American Heart Association — Nation of Lifesavers / Heart Club Grants

**What they fund:** CPR training and AED resources for high schools and colleges. Provides CPR in Schools Kits (manikins, AED simulators, training materials) plus $500 in funding per Heart Club. No prior CPR credentialing required.

**Why this matters for you:** If the nonprofit's mission includes bringing CPR/first aid training to high school EMS programs, AHA's free resources are complementary. The nonprofit could help high schools establish Heart Clubs as a pipeline into its EMS education programs.

**Typical award sizes:** In-kind training kits plus $500 cash per school Heart Club. AHA also has Community Health Subawards ranging $2,500-$10,000 (funded regionally with partners like Blue Cross). Social Impact Funds: $150,000-$500,000 for social entrepreneurs addressing health equity.

**Eligibility:** Schools, community organizations, nonprofits. Very accessible for new organizations.

**Application process:** Varies by program. Heart Club grants through AHA regional offices. Social Impact Funds are competitive and require demonstrated innovation.

**Realistic assessment:** HIGH for Heart Club partnerships and training resources. LOW-MODERATE for Social Impact Funds (requires demonstrated entrepreneurial track record). Good strategy: leverage AHA training resources while pursuing the nonprofit's own EMS education curriculum.

**Sources:** [AHA CPR in Schools](https://cpr.heart.org/en/training-programs/community-programs) | [AHA Social Impact Funds](https://www.heart.org/en/aha-ventures/social-impact-funds/what-we-fund)

### Hero Fund America

**What they fund:** Education, training, life-saving equipment, and mental health needs of first responders.

**Typical award sizes:** Up to $1,500 per grant.

**Application process:** 2026 First Responders Grant Round accepts applications throughout June.

**Realistic assessment:** HIGH but small. Easy to get, good for building a grant track record even though the amount is nominal. Apply every year.

### State Farm — Good Neighbor Citizenship Grants / NVFC Firefighter Safety Program

**What they fund:** Two relevant tracks: (1) Safety grants for disaster preparedness, home safety, fire prevention, teen driver education, and auto/roadway safety; (2) Through partnership with NVFC, $10,000 equipment grants to 150 volunteer fire departments annually ($1.5M total commitment in 2025).

**Why this matters for you:** The safety grant track could fund community first aid education or teen safety programming. The NVFC partnership is equipment-focused.

**Typical award sizes:** Varies. The NVFC program is $10,000 per department. Community grants are generally $5,000-$25,000 based on publicly available data.

**Eligibility:** 501(c)(3) organizations, educational institutions, government entities, 501(c)(4) volunteer fire companies. New organizations are eligible.

**Application process:** Primarily invitation-based, but unsolicited requests for programs meeting focus areas can be submitted through the State Farm community grants portal. Education funding is directed toward K-12 and STEM, primarily near major State Farm employment hubs.

**Realistic assessment:** LOW-MODERATE. State Farm's community grants lean toward their employment hub communities and established partners. The NVFC partnership is for volunteer fire departments, not nonprofits. Worth submitting an inquiry but don't build your strategy around it.

**Sources:** [State Farm Community Grants](https://www.statefarm.com/about-us/corporate-responsibility/community-grants) | [NVFC Good Neighbor Program](https://www.nvfc.org/good-neighbor-firefighter-safety-program/)

### Georgia Health Foundation

**What they fund:** Georgia nonprofits addressing community health through direct services, education, advocacy, or policy. Six focus areas: behavioral health, direct health services, health advocacy, innovative approaches, nutrition/wellness, rural health access.

**Typical award sizes:** $10,000-$30,000.

**Eligibility:** Must be a Georgia-based 501(c)(3) serving Georgia residents with capacity to implement, evaluate, and report on funded activities.

**Critical limitation:** Does NOT provide startup or seed funding. Will consider pilot programs from organizations with proven track records.

**Application timeline:** June 1 deadline. Initial review in July. Decisions in August. Notifications in September.

**Realistic assessment:** LOW for Year 1 (explicitly excludes startups). MODERATE for Year 2+ once the nonprofit has a track record. The $10K-$30K range is meaningful for a young nonprofit. Target this for Year 2 with documented program outcomes from Year 1.

**Sources:** [Georgia Health Foundation Grants](https://www.gahealthfdn.org/grants)

## TIER 2: ACHIEVABLE WITH 1-3 YEARS OF TRACK RECORD

These funders are realistic targets once the nonprofit has documented outcomes and a financial history.

### Robert Wood Johnson Foundation (RWJF)

**What they fund:** Health equity, community health, workforce development, data-driven innovation, structural change. The nation's largest philanthropy focused exclusively on health.

**Relevant current programs:**
- **Local Data for Equitable Communities:** Up to 30 grants of $50,000 each for nonprofits collecting/analyzing data to address inequities. Open to 501(c)(3) and 501(c)(4) organizations. Deadline: March 3, 2026.
- **Research to Advance Racial and Indigenous Health Equity:** $50,000-$200,000. Community-based organizations can lead (universities cannot).
- **From Insight to Action (upcoming April 2026):** Action-oriented, community-centered research advancing health equity.
- **Exploring Equitable Futures:** Seeds unconventional ideas that could advance health equity. Annual cycle, closes ~October 15, reopens ~February 1.

**Minimum budget requirement:** No explicit minimum. However, RWJF's Program Finance team conducts financial review of the applicant organization's financial statements, tax forms, audits, and other data. They evaluate "organizational infrastructure that demonstrates sufficient capacity and history."

**Application process:** Competitive calls for proposals with specific eligibility criteria. Some programs accept LOIs; others require full proposals. Sign up for funding alerts at rwjf.org.

**Realistic assessment:** LOW for Year 1 — RWJF evaluates organizational capacity and financial history, which a brand-new nonprofit lacks. MODERATE-HIGH for Years 2-3 with documented outcomes. The "Exploring Equitable Futures" program is the most accessible since it explicitly seeks unconventional ideas. The "Local Data for Equitable Communities" program could work if the nonprofit can demonstrate data collection around EMS education access disparities.

**Sources:** [RWJF Grants](https://www.rwjf.org/en/grants.html) | [Active Opportunities](https://www.rwjf.org/en/grants/active-funding-opportunities.html)

### The Hearst Foundations

**What they fund:** Education (higher ed, science education, STEM career pathways) and Health (regional hospitals, medical centers, healthcare workforce programs designed to enhance skills and increase practitioners/educators).

**Critical requirement:** Only funds organizations with annual operating budgets of at least $1 million. 80% of education and health grantees have budgets of $10M or higher.

**Minimum grant size:** $100,000.

**Application process:** Year-round rolling applications through the Hearst Foundations Grants Portal, no deadlines. Evaluation spans 4-6 weeks. Board meets quarterly. Average time from submission to award: 6 months. Must wait 3 years after an award before reapplying; 1 year after a decline.

**Realistic assessment:** NOT REALISTIC for a new nonprofit. The $1M operating budget minimum is a hard barrier. This becomes relevant only when the organization reaches significant scale, likely 5+ years out. Worth noting for long-term strategic planning but not actionable now.

**Sources:** [Hearst Foundations FAQ](https://www.hearstfdn.org/faq/) | [How to Apply](https://www.hearstfdn.org/applying-reporting/how-to-apply/) | [Education Priorities](https://www.hearstfdn.org/education/funding-priorities)

### John A. Hartford Foundation

**What they fund:** Improving care for older adults through three strategic areas: (1) Age-Friendly Health Systems, (2) Family Caregivers, (3) Serious Illness and End-of-Life Care. Has invested over $656M in aging and health since 1982.

**End-of-life focus areas:** Increasing access to palliative care services, workforce education and preparation, communication/community-based solutions, and public policy. Recent grants focus on value-based payment models, community-based palliative care expansion, and diagnostic excellence.

**Also funds:** Geriatric Workforce Enhancement Program (GWEP) National Coordinating Center through the American Geriatrics Society, leveraging HRSA federal funding.

**How to engage:** The foundation funds through strategic partnerships and targeted solicitations. There is a grant search tool on their website, but most funding flows through established intermediaries (AGS, CAPC, IHI) rather than open calls.

**Typical award sizes:** Recent board approvals totaled $17M across multiple grants. Individual grants range from $100K to multi-million. This is large-scale systems-change philanthropy.

**Realistic assessment:** NOT REALISTIC for direct funding of a new nonprofit. Hartford funds through established national intermediaries. However, the nonprofit should monitor Hartford-funded initiatives (especially GWEP sites and Age-Friendly Health Systems) for partnership opportunities. If the nonprofit develops hospice education programs, aligning with Hartford's framework (especially 4M model: What Matters, Medication, Mentation, Mobility) strengthens credibility with other funders.

**Sources:** [Hartford Foundation Grants](https://www.johnahartford.org/grants-strategy) | [Serious Illness Strategy](https://www.johnahartford.org/grants-strategy/current-strategies/serious-illness-end-of-life-care) | Contact: 212.832.7788

### ZOLL Foundation

**What they fund:** "Seed" grants for medical research in emergency medicine, critical care, trauma, cardiovascular health, and related fields. Focus on young researchers and translational research enhancing training and clinical delivery.

**Typical award sizes:** Starting at $25,000, sometimes exceeding $100,000. Over $9M awarded to 195 grantees since 2014.

**Application process:** Applications reviewed by Board twice annually with deadlines of March 31 and September 30.

**Realistic assessment:** LOW-MODERATE. ZOLL Foundation targets individual researchers, not organizational operations. However, if the nonprofit develops a research component (e.g., studying outcomes of free EMS education access in underserved communities), a seed grant application could work. More relevant for Years 2-3 when the nonprofit has data to propose research around.

**Sources:** [ZOLL Foundation](https://www.zollfoundation.org/) | [Funded Projects](https://www.zollfoundation.org/projects/)

### Helmsley Charitable Trust — Rural Healthcare

**What they fund:** Innovative rural healthcare projects using technology to connect patients to emergency care, bring therapies to remote areas, and train rural hospital/EMS personnel. Over $750M awarded across 694 grants. Has funded 25,000 AEDs for law enforcement, nearly 400 LUCAS mechanical CPR devices for rural hospitals, and the first mobile ECMO unit.

**CRITICAL LIMITATION — Geographic:** Only funds in Hawaii, Iowa, Minnesota, Montana, Nebraska, Nevada, North Dakota, South Dakota, Wyoming, and two U.S. Pacific territories. Georgia is NOT included.

**CRITICAL LIMITATION — Application:** Invitation-only grantmaking. Does not accept unsolicited proposals. In-house experts seek partners. Occasional RFPs with narrow scope.

**Realistic assessment:** NOT APPLICABLE. Georgia is outside Helmsley's geographic focus, and the model is invitation-only. Do not pursue.

**Sources:** [Helmsley Rural Healthcare](https://helmsleytrust.org/our-focus-areas/rural-healthcare/) | Contact: grants@helmsleytrust.org

### Arthur Vining Davis Foundations — Palliative Care

**What they fund:** Palliative care research and access, including pediatric palliative care, patients' spiritual needs, improved access, and curricula/standards development. Made the first grant to Connecticut Hospice (1976), launching America's hospice movement. Recent grant to CAPC exceeded $500K.

**CRITICAL LIMITATION:** Invitation-only since 2021. "We do not accept unsolicited funding requests in this Program Area." Palliative care work is exclusively through existing partnerships.

**Other AVDF programs:** The foundation does accept applications in other program areas (education, religion), with grant ranges of $25,000-$350,000. The 2026-2027 review cycle information to be announced January 2026.

**Realistic assessment:** NOT APPLICABLE for palliative care (invitation-only). POSSIBLE for education programs if the nonprofit frames its EMS education work under AVDF's broader education priorities. Monitor for 2027 cycle announcements.

**Sources:** [AVDF Palliative Care](https://www.avdf.org/programs-overview/palliative-care/) | [Grant Calendar](https://www.avdf.org/grant-seekers/grant-calendar/)

### Stryker

**What they fund:** Educational grants and charitable contributions supporting medical technology advancement and patient care. Fellowship programs for EMS medical directors (24-month, $80,000, through NAEMSP partnership). Research grants through their portal.

**How to access:** Grants and donations portal at stryker.com. Fellowship applications close March 1, 2026 for 2027 grants.

**Realistic assessment for organizational grants:** LOW. Stryker's giving focuses on medical education for practitioners, research grants, and fellowships for existing clinicians. Not a natural fit for a nonprofit providing free EMS education tools to high school students. However, Stryker's employee matching gift program could be valuable if board members or donors are Stryker employees.

**Product partnership potential:** MODERATE. Stryker manufactures EMS training equipment. A product donation or discounted-purchase arrangement for the nonprofit's training programs could be more realistic than a cash grant. Approach their corporate social responsibility team about in-kind support.

**Sources:** [Stryker Grants](https://www.stryker.com/us/en/interventional-spine/grants-and-donations.html) | [Stryker Research Grants](https://www.researchgrants.stryker.com/)

However, an AED distribution partnership (like their RCX Sports model) could be compelling — the nonprofit could propose deploying AEDs/training equipment to high school EMS programs with ZOLL branding.

### Laerdal Medical

**What they fund:** Laerdal is the world's leading manufacturer of healthcare simulation and training equipment (CPR manikins, AED trainers, patient simulators). They provide grant-writing resources and support organizations seeking funding for Laerdal training products.

**Laerdal Global Health:** Their nonprofit arm works to save lives of mothers and newborns in low-resource settings. Focused on developing countries, not U.S. domestic programs.

**Realistic assessment for the nonprofit:** HIGH for product partnerships. Laerdal is the most natural corporate partner for an EMS education nonprofit. Their products ARE the training tools the nonprofit would use. Approach them about:
- Discounted or donated training manikins and AED trainers for high school programs
- Co-branded education initiatives
- Their grant-writing assistance to help fund Laerdal equipment purchases
- Sponsorship of the nonprofit's programs

Laerdal has a strong mission alignment ("helping save lives") and a history of supporting EMS education. This should be one of the first corporate conversations.

## KEY STRATEGIC INSIGHTS

1. **The grant landscape for a new 501(c)(3) is honestly thin in Year 1.** Most major foundations require track record. The strategy must be: win small grants first (Firehouse Subs, Hero Fund, local community foundations), document outcomes aggressively, then scale to larger funders.

2. **Corporate partnerships may be more valuable than cash grants early on.** Laerdal, ZOLL, and Stryker product donations/partnerships can provide the training equipment the nonprofit needs to run programs, which then generates the outcomes data needed to win cash grants.

3. **The EMS education niche is more fundable than it first appears** — but you have to frame it as "healthcare workforce pipeline" or "health equity" rather than just "EMS training." Funders like RWJF and Kaiser Permanente respond to health equity and workforce development language.

4. **The hospice/end-of-life care education program has fewer accessible funders** than the EMS education program in the early years. The major palliative care funders (Hartford, AVDF, Cambia) either fund through intermediaries, are invitation-only, or are geographically restricted. Strategy: build the hospice program on the EMS education program's credibility, and pursue hospice-specific funding once the organization has 2+ years of track record.

5. **Georgia-specific funders are critical for early credibility.** Georgia Health Foundation, Community Foundation for Greater Atlanta, Georgia's Own Foundation, and Kaiser Permanente Georgia should be primary targets. Being a Georgia-based organization serving Georgia communities is an advantage in these pools.

6. **Federal funding (Perkins V, HRSA, FEMA AFG) may be more accessible than private foundations** for a new organization, especially if partnering with established entities like school districts or fire departments. See the main `grant-landscape.md` for federal program details.
